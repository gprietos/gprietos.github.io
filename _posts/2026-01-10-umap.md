# Overview


Much of the data we deal with day to day lives in way more dimensions than we can picture. An image is usually made of thousands of pixel values, and a sentence passed through a language model becomes a vector of hundreds of numbers. Our intuition is built for a three-dimensional world, so once we move into higher dimensions we can no longer see or even conceptualize what the data looks like, or what shape it takes

That's where dimensionality reduction techniques come in. The idea is to project the data down to two or three dimensions so that the result still resembles the structure of the original data as much as possible. Once we are able to see the data, we can easily spot patterns present in it like clusters, outliers, ... And this is not only about plotting, as a lower-dimensional version of the data can also be useful for tasks like clustering or classification.

There are many methods for dimensionality reduction, like PCA or t-SNE. This post focuses on **UMAP**, one of the most popular choices for visualizing high-dimensional data. It's fast, it handles large datasets well and it tends to keep both the fine details and the overall structure of the data. Also, I find the math behind it and how each step is justified really interesting 🙂. We'll cover the fundamentals behind it, go step by step through how it works, and look at some of its limitations.

<strong style="color: #e0a800">⚠ Warning!</strong> The theory can get a bit dense if you're not familiar with this kind of topics. Almost each step comes with an animation to help make sense of it, but if you're not that interested in the explanation, feel free to skip ahead to [UMAP Examples](#umap-examples) and see it in action.

If you want to play around with it yourself, the code behind the examples is in [umap-examples](https://github.com/gprietos/umap-examples). It's just a few simple scripts to try things out, nothing too fancy.

# Topological Data Analysis and Simplicial Complexes

High-dimensional data rarely fills the space it lives in. Instead, the data tends to lie close to a simpler shape with fewer dimensions embedded in $\mathbb{R}^n$, called a **manifold**. Seen up close at any of its points, the manifold looks like flat Euclidean space. As we almost never know this shape in advance, the first thing UMAP does is approximate it from the data points themselves[^umap-paper].

**Topological data analysis** (TDA) studies the shape of that manifold through its **topological** features. **Homology** is the mathematical tool used to detect and count structural features like loops, voids, and connected components. Because these features are invariant when the shape is stretched or bent, and only change if it is torn or glued, they provide a robust description of a manifold's structure. This structure is precisely what UMAP aims to preserve when projecting high-dimensional data into lower dimensions.

To describe a shape, we approximate it using simple building blocks. Surfaces are typically approximated using **triangles**, as any polygonal region can be broken down into triangles through triangulation. Triangles generalize to any dimension, and these generalizations are called **simplices**.

A fundamental idea in TDA is to glue simplices together along their faces (simplices on the boundary of a simplex) to approximate the shape of the data. The resulting structure is called a **simplicial complex**, and, when built well, it can capture the topology of the space it approximates.

## Building a simplicial complex

Since we only have discrete data samples and not the true underlying manifold, we need to approximate its topology. One common way to do this is to place a ball of radius $\epsilon$ around each data point and add a simplex for every group of points whose balls overlap. Depending on how overlap is defined, we get two different complexes[^cech-rips]:


- **Čech complex** $C_\epsilon$: $k+1$ points form a $k$-simplex if all their balls share a common point.
- **Vietoris–Rips complex** $V_\epsilon$: $k+1$ points form a $k$-simplex if their balls overlap pairwise, in other words, if all pairwise distances are $\leq 2\epsilon$.

Why should a complex built this way say anything about the shape of the data? The balls fill in the space between the samples, so together they form a solid region that approximates the manifold. The **Nerve theorem**[^hatcher] tells us that the Čech complex has the same shape as that **union of the balls**[^umap-docs]. Put together, if the balls cover the manifold well enough, the complex captures the shape of the manifold. This is the guarantee behind the whole construction, and it also shows its limit. The complex is only as good as the balls, so if they miss part of the manifold, or bridge across a hole or between separate parts, the complex will reproduce that mistake. The Vietoris–Rips complex has no exact guarantee of this kind, but it stays close to the Čech complex, so the same intuition applies.

Whether the balls cover the manifold well depends on their size, which makes the radius $\epsilon$ the critical choice. As $\epsilon$ grows, simplices are only ever added, so each complex keeps every simplex of the previous one: $V_{\epsilon_1} \subseteq V_{\epsilon_2} \subseteq \dots \subseteq V_{\epsilon_n}$ for $\epsilon_1 < \epsilon_2 < \dots < \epsilon_n$. This nested sequence is called a **filtration**. **Persistent homology** tracks features along the filtration and keeps those that persist over a wide range of $\epsilon$, treating short-lived ones as noise. UMAP does not rely on persistent homology. It answers the question of which radius to choose in its own way, as we will see later.

The Čech complex faithfully tracks the union of the balls, but checking higher-order overlaps is computationally expensive. The Vietoris–Rips complex offers a practical alternative by relying solely on pairwise distances, meaning its structure is entirely determined by its **1-skeleton**: its vertices and edges (0- and 1-simplices)[^umap-docs]. The whole complex reduces the topological representation of the data to a **graph**[^umap-paper], and this graph is what UMAP works with. Finding a low-dimensional representation of the data then simplifies to laying out this graph in a lower-dimensional target space.

# UMAP Algorithm


The **UMAP algorithm** consists of two main steps:
1. **Graph construction** –  Construct a weighted graph similar to the Vietoris–Rips complex, but in which each point is connected to its $k$ nearest neighbors.
2. **Graph Layout Optimization** – Optimize a low-dimensional embedding that preserves the topological structure of the original graph.


## Graph construction


### Choosing a radius

What radius should we pick to construct a simplicial complex that approximates the manifold on which the data lies? The answer depends on how the data is spread over the manifold.

If the data were uniformly distributed over the manifold and densely sampled, every ball would capture roughly the same number of neighbors. Balls of a single well-chosen radius would then cover the whole manifold, and the simplicial complex built from them would approximate its topology[^umap-docs].


Real-world data is rarely like that. It is scattered, with dense and sparse regions, so a ball with the same radius captures a different number of neighbors for each point.

Where the data is sparse, some points or small groups of points end up **isolated**. Their balls do not overlap the rest, so the complex breaks into separate pieces even though the underlying manifold is connected there.

Where the data is dense, the balls **bunch** together and overlap so much that they form high-dimensional simplices. These can bridge across holes or between parts of the manifold that should stay separate, filling them in.

In both cases the union of the balls no longer matches the manifold, and since, as the Nerve theorem states, the complex reproduces the shape of that union, the topology it captures is wrong. The problem is that both failures can happen at the same time, so a single radius is often not enough to avoid them.

</div>


### A distance for each point

UMAP turns the problem around. Instead of hoping the data is uniformly distributed, it **assumes** it is. This is the **first assumption** UMAP is built on[^umap-paper]. If the data looks non-uniform but we assume it is uniform, then what must vary is the notion of distance itself. The manifold gets its own way of measuring distance, different from the one inherited from $\mathbb{R}^n$, chosen so that the data is uniformly distributed with respect to it. Consequently, space is stretched where points are dense and shrunk where they are sparse[^umap-docs].

<div style="width: 75%; margin: 0 auto;" markdown="1">
{% include embed/video.html src='/assets/videos/umap/UniformManifold3D.mp4' title="Under the manifold's own distance, the dense region is enlarged and the sparse one shrunk, so the same points end up uniformly distributed." %}
</div>
<!-- <span style="color: red">Video Uniform Manifold 3d</span> -->

How do we measure distance under this assumption? In geometry, a way of measuring distance on a manifold is called a **metric**, and a manifold equipped with one is a **Riemannian manifold**. The distance between two points measured along the manifold with this metric is their **geodesic distance**. A result from the UMAP paper[^umap-paper] shows that this distance can be approximated locally. Suppose the metric is roughly constant in a small region around a point $p$, and take a ball centered at $p$ whose volume, measured with the manifold's metric, is that of a unit ball. For any point $q$ inside it, the geodesic distance is the ambient distance scaled by $1/r$,

$$
d_{M}(p,q) \approx \frac{1}{r}\, d_{\mathbb{R}^n}(p,q)
$$

where $r$ is the radius of that same ball and $d_{\mathbb{R}^n}$ is the distance, both measured in the ambient space $\mathbb{R}^n$.

If the data is uniformly distributed on the manifold with respect to its metric, then, away from any boundaries, any ball of fixed volume on the manifold contains approximately the same number of points, regardless of where it is located. Equivalently, balls centered at each data point $x_i$ that contain exactly its $k$ nearest neighbors all have approximately the same volume on the manifold. Seen from $\mathbb{R}^n$, however, these balls have different sizes. The radius $r_i$ of the ball around $x_i$ is the distance from $x_i$ to its $k$-th nearest neighbor, and dividing ambient distances by it approximates the geodesic distance from $x_i$ to its neighbors. This gives **each point its own distance function**,

$$
d_i(x_i,x_j) = \frac{1}{r_i}\, d_{\mathbb{R}^n}(x_i,x_j)
$$


Where the data is dense $r_i$ is small, so distances measured from $x_i$ are stretched, and where it is sparse $r_i$ is large, so they shrink. In practice, **the ball around each point stretches or shrinks to reach its $k$ nearest neighbors**.

With that, the problem of choosing a radius is replaced by **choosing the number of neighbors $k$**. Instead of an **$\epsilon$-neighborhood** approach, where every point has a ball of the same radius $\epsilon$, UMAP uses a **$k$-nearest neighbor** approach, where each ball adapts to the data around it[^brunton]. This is easier since a good radius depends on the scale of each dataset and would differ from region to region, while the same $k$ works everywhere and across different datasets. Which value of $k$ to choose and how it changes the result will be discussed later in [Effect of k and min_dist hyperparameters](#effect-of-k-and-min_dist-hyperparameters).


### Edge weights

With a local distance, we can say not only *whether* a point is a neighbor but also *how close* it is. Membership in a ball becomes the **weight of the edge**, a value between 0 and 1 that decays exponentially with distance, high near the center and low near the edge[^umap-docs].

In practice, this can still leave points almost isolated with every edge weight close to zero. UMAP avoids this with a **second assumption**[^umap-paper], that the manifold is **locally connected**. Concretely, every point is connected with full confidence to at least its nearest neighbor. That edge gets weight 1, and the weights of the other neighbors only start decaying **after the nearest neighbor**[^umap-docs].

Local connectivity keeps full weight out to the nearest neighbor, so no point is left isolated." %}
</div>

Local connectivity also helps with the **curse of dimensionality**. In high dimensions, distances tend to be large and similar to one another. Subtracting the distance to the nearest neighbor removes that shared offset, so the weights depend on the *differences* between neighbors rather than on their absolute distances, which barely distinguish them[^umap-docs].



Putting the pieces together, the weight of the edge from $x_i$ to its $j$-th nearest neighbor $x_{i_j}$ is[^umap-paper]

$$
w_{j \mid i} = \exp\left(-\frac{\max\left(0,\; d(x_i, x_{i_j}) - \rho_i\right)}{\sigma_i}\right)
$$

where $\rho_i$ is the distance to the nearest neighbor, the local connectivity offset,

$$
\rho_i = \min\left\{\, d(x_i, x_{i_j}) \;:\; 1 \le j \le k,\ d(x_i, x_{i_j}) > 0 \,\right\}
$$

and $\sigma_i$ is a per-point normalization factor that sets how fast the weights decay. It acts like $r_i$ in the distance function $d_i$, as it stretches or shrinks the distances measured from $x_i$, which is how it defines the local metric around that point. It is found by searching for the value that makes the weights of the $k$ neighbors add up to $\log_2(k)$, a target chosen empirically[^umap-paper].

$$
\sum_{j=1}^{k} w_{j \mid i} = \log_2(k)
$$

### Merging the local views

Each point now has weighted edges to its $k$ nearest neighbors, measured with its own local metric, which form a **local view** of the data around it. Combined, these views form a **directed weighted graph** where edge weights and neighbor relationships are **asymmetric**[^umap-paper]. It can be represented as a **weight matrix**, with $w_{j \mid i}$ and $w_{i \mid j}$ in mirrored positions.

To reconcile these incompatible local views, UMAP models each one as a **fuzzy simplicial set**, a simplicial complex in which every simplex has a membership strength between 0 and 1, in our case given by the edge weights. These sets are merged with a **fuzzy union** that treats each weight as the **probability that the edge exists**, and computes the combined weight as the probability that *at least one* of the two directed edges exists[^umap-paper],

$$
w_{ij} = 1 - (1 - w_{j \mid i})(1 - w_{i \mid j}) = w_{j \mid i} + w_{i \mid j} - w_{j \mid i}\, w_{i \mid j}
$$

The fuzzy union ensures that the merged weight is at least as large as either of the two, so a connection that one point is confident about is never weakened by the other.

Applied to all pairs of points, this yields a single **undirected weighted graph** which captures the topology of the data's underlying manifold. Its weight matrix is now symmetric, with $w_{ij} = w_{ji}$. Note that the result is no longer a $k$-nearest neighbors graph, as points can gain extra edges from others that count them as neighbors.

### Graph construction summary

1. Find the $k$ nearest neighbors of each point.
2. Give each point its own distance so that the data is uniform on the manifold, offset by the distance to its nearest neighbor $\rho_i$ and scaled by $\sigma_i$.
3. Turn these distances into edge weights that decay exponentially, giving each point a weighted local view.
4. Merge the local views with the fuzzy union into a single undirected weighted graph.




## Graph layout optimization

The weighted graph $H$ we just built captures the topology of the data in high dimensions. The goal now is to embed the data into a low-dimensional space $\mathbb{R}^d$ so that its low-dimensional embedding keeps the same topology. This is achieved by making a weighted graph $L$ of the embedded points resemble $H$ as closely as possible.

These points could start from random positions, but UMAP uses a better starting layout, described in [Implementation Details](#implementation-details), and then moves them step by step.

The points move step by step until the weights of L match those of H. The three clusters end up clearly separated along the line." %}
</div>



### A fixed distance in the low-dimensional space

In high dimensions the manifold was unknown, so UMAP had to estimate it, assuming the data is uniformly distributed over it, by giving each point its own local distance. In the low-dimensional space, the manifold is just $\mathbb{R}^d$ itself, where distance is measured the same way everywhere. Consequently, the weights of $L$ depend only on how far apart two points are, in the same way for every pair.

This also makes $L$ symmetric from the start. Unlike the high-dimensional graph, which needed the fuzzy union to merge its asymmetric weights, both directions of a pair here depend on the same distance, so they always agree.



### min_dist and the target curve

The graph construction also assumed the manifold is **locally connected**, giving every point full weight to its nearest neighbor. The low-dimensional embedding should keep this idea, but the distance to the nearest neighbor $\rho_i$ was a per-point distance, and now distance is measured the same way for every pair. 

Instead, UMAP uses a **hyperparameter** that sets the expected distance between nearest neighbors in the low-dimensional embedding, the **minimum distance** `min_dist`. Pairs closer than `min_dist` are considered fully connected and beyond it the weight decays exponentially, just like the high-dimensional weights decayed beyond $\rho_i$[^tilestats],

$$
\Psi(d) =
\begin{cases}
1 & \text{if } d \le \text{min\_dist} \\
\exp\left(-(d - \text{min\_dist})\right) & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
$$

where $d = \lVert y_i - y_j \rVert_2$ is the Euclidean distance between two embedded points. 

This gives the weight of each pair in $L$, $v_{ij} = \Psi(\lVert y_i - y_j \rVert_2)$.

How the choice of `min_dist` changes the final low-dimensional embedding is covered in [Effect of k and min_dist hyperparameters](#effect-of-k-and-min_dist-hyperparameters).

### Comparing the two graphs

<!-- ![[IMG-20260416125234808.png|right|400]] -->

Both graphs have the same nodes, one per data point, so we can compare them pair by pair. Points strongly connected in $H$ should stay strongly connected in $L$, and points with no edge in $H$ should end up far apart. 

They have **different numbers of edges**, as $H$ only connects near neighbors while $L$ gives every pair a weight. Comparing only the edges of $H$ is therefore not enough, and the pairs with no edge need to be compared too.

Since each weight can be interpreted as the probability that the edge exists, every pair defines a **Bernoulli variable** in each graph, with two outcomes, edge or no edge, and probability $w_{ij}$ in $H$ and $v_{ij}$ in $L$, so the natural way to compare them is the **KL divergence** between the two distributions of each pair[^umap-paper][^umap-docs],

$$
C = \sum_{i < j} \bigg[ \underbrace{w_{ij} \log\frac{w_{ij}}{v_{ij}}}_{\substack{\text{high } w_{ij} \\ \downarrow \\ \text{pull points together}}} + \underbrace{(1 - w_{ij}) \log\frac{1 - w_{ij}}{1 - v_{ij}}}_{\substack{\text{low } w_{ij} \\ \downarrow \\ \text{push points apart}}} \bigg]
$$

where the sum runs over each pair of points once (the upper triangle of each weight matrix[^tilestats]), $w_{ij}$ is the weight of the pair in $H$ from the fuzzy union (0 if they are not connected), and $v_{ij}$ its weight in $L$.

Expanding the logarithms splits $C$ into two sums. The first sum depends only on $w_{ij}$, which is fixed during the optimization, so minimizing $C$ comes down to minimizing the second term, the **binary cross-entropy** between $w_{ij}$ and $v_{ij}$[^umap-paper],

$$
\operatorname*{argmin}_{Y}\, C = \operatorname*{argmin}_{Y} \left( \enclose{southeastarrow}{\sum_{i < j} \left[ w_{ij} \log w_{ij} + (1 - w_{ij}) \log(1 - w_{ij}) \right]} - \sum_{i < j} \left[ w_{ij} \log v_{ij} + (1 - w_{ij}) \log(1 - v_{ij}) \right] \right)
$$

To minimize it, UMAP moves the points $y_i$ with **gradient descent**, nudging each one in the direction that lowers the loss the most.



### A smooth curve

Gradient descent needs the derivative of the loss, but $\Psi$ is not differentiable at `min_dist`, where its flat part meets the exponential decay with a sharp corner. UMAP therefore approximates it with a smooth curve of similar shape[^tilestats],

$$
\Phi(d) = \frac{1}{1 + a\, d^{2b}}
$$

$a$ controls the **width** of the curve, with larger values making it drop sooner, and $b$ its **shape**, with larger values giving a flatter top and a steeper drop, closer to $\Psi$.

Both parameters are obtained with a non-linear least-squares fit of $\Phi$ to $\Psi$ for the given `min_dist`[^umap-paper]. From here on, the weight of a pair in $L$ is $v_{ij} = \Phi(d_{ij})$.

### Attraction and repulsion

In practice, UMAP lays out the points with a **force-directed graph layout**, which applies attractive forces along the edges of $H$ and repulsive forces between every pair of points that is not fully connected[^umap-paper]. These forces are the gradients of the two terms of the loss. Writing $d_{ij} = \lVert y_i - y_j \rVert_2$, the loss for a single pair is

$$
-\, w_{ij} \log \Phi(d_{ij}) \;-\; (1 - w_{ij}) \log\big(1 - \Phi(d_{ij})\big)
$$

The first term is weighted by $w_{ij}$ and gets smaller as $v_{ij}$ grows, that is, as the points get closer. Its gradient gives the **attractive force**[^umap-paper], and each gradient descent step, which moves against it, **pulls** $y_i$ towards $y_j$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\partial}{\partial y_i}\Big[-w_{ij}\log\Phi(d_{ij})\Big]
&= \frac{\partial}{\partial y_i}\Big[w_{ij}\log\big(1 + a\, d_{ij}^{2b}\big)\Big]
= w_{ij}\, \frac{1}{1 + a\, d_{ij}^{2b}}\; \frac{\partial}{\partial y_i}\Big[a \big(d_{ij}^2\big)^{b}\Big] \\
&= w_{ij}\, \frac{1}{1 + a\, d_{ij}^{2b}}\; a\, b \big(d_{ij}^2\big)^{b-1}\, \frac{\partial d_{ij}^2}{\partial y_i}
= w_{ij}\, \frac{a\, b\, d_{ij}^{2(b-1)}}{1 + a\, d_{ij}^{2b}}\; 2\,(y_i - y_j) \\
&= \frac{2ab\, d_{ij}^{2(b-1)}}{1 + a\, d_{ij}^{2b}}\; w_{ij}\, (y_i - y_j)
\end{aligned}
$$

The second term is weighted by $1 - w_{ij}$, so it is strongest for the pairs with no edge, and gets smaller as $v_{ij}$ shrinks, that is, as the points move apart. Its gradient gives the **repulsive force**[^umap-paper], and each gradient descent step, which moves against it, pushes $y_i$ **away from** $y_j$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\partial}{\partial y_i}\Big[-(1 - w_{ij})\log\big(1 - \Phi(d_{ij})\big)\Big]
&= -(1 - w_{ij})\, \frac{\partial}{\partial y_i}\left[\log\frac{a\, d_{ij}^{2b}}{1 + a\, d_{ij}^{2b}}\right] \\
&= -(1 - w_{ij})\, \frac{\partial}{\partial y_i}\Big[\log a + b \log d_{ij}^2 - \log\big(1 + a \big(d_{ij}^2\big)^{b}\big)\Big] \\
&= -(1 - w_{ij}) \left(\frac{b}{d_{ij}^2} - \frac{a\, b \big(d_{ij}^2\big)^{b-1}}{1 + a\, d_{ij}^{2b}}\right) \frac{\partial d_{ij}^2}{\partial y_i} \\
&= -(1 - w_{ij})\, \frac{b}{d_{ij}^2\left(1 + a\, d_{ij}^{2b}\right)}\; 2\,(y_i - y_j) \\
&= -\frac{2b}{d_{ij}^2\left(1 + a\, d_{ij}^{2b}\right)}\; (1 - w_{ij})\, (y_i - y_j)
\end{aligned}
$$


<!-- In practice a small $\varepsilon = 0.001$ is added to $d_{ij}^2$ in the denominator to avoid dividing by zero when two points overlap[^umap-paper][^umap-learn]. -->

Combining both forces and using $v_{ij} = 1/(1 + a\, d_{ij}^{2b})$, the gradient with respect to $y_i$ becomes

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\partial C}{\partial y_i}
&= \sum_{j \ne i} \bigg[ \underbrace{\frac{2ab\, d_{ij}^{2(b-1)}}{1 + a\, d_{ij}^{2b}}\; w_{ij}}_{\text{attraction}} \;-\; \underbrace{\frac{2b}{d_{ij}^2\left(1 + a\, d_{ij}^{2b}\right)}\; (1 - w_{ij})}_{\text{repulsion}} \bigg] (y_i - y_j) \\
&= \sum_{j \ne i} 2ab\, \frac{v_{ij}\,(w_{ij} - v_{ij})}{1 - v_{ij}}\; d_{ij}^{2(b-1)}\, (y_i - y_j)
\end{aligned}
$$

In the combined form, a pair that is weaker in $L$ than in $H$ ($v_{ij} < w_{ij}$) is pulled together, a pair that is stronger ($v_{ij} > w_{ij}$) is pushed apart, and its forces balance once both weights agree. Following these forces, the points move until the weights of $L$ match those of $H$ as closely as possible, and with them the topology of the data[^umap-paper].

The two forces also scale differently with distance. The attraction stays bounded and fades slowly with distance, while the repulsion blows up at short range and fades quickly. Neighbors in $H$ therefore settle at around `min_dist` instead of collapsing onto each other. Points with no edge are pushed apart only while they are close, but nothing pulls them back, so unconnected groups drift into separate clusters.

### Running the optimization

Gradient descent applies these forces iteratively. At each step, every point moves against its gradient by an amount set by the **learning rate** $\alpha$,

$$
y_i \leftarrow y_i - \alpha \frac{\partial C}{\partial y_i}
$$

Computing this gradient over all pairs is expensive, so in practice UMAP approximates it by sampling, which turns the optimization into **stochastic gradient descent**, as described in [Implementation Details](#implementation-details).

The optimization does not run until the points settle. It runs for a fixed number of iterations while $\alpha$ slowly decays, so early steps make large moves that shape the global layout, and later ones only refine local neighborhoods[^embedding-room]. The layout gradually freezes into its final shape, much like the cooling schedule of simulated annealing[^umap-paper].




### Graph layout summary

1. Place the points in $\mathbb{R}^d$, using the standard Euclidean distance for every pair.
2. Turn distances into weights with a curve that is flat up to `min_dist` and decays beyond it, approximated by the smooth $\Phi(d) = 1/(1 + a\, d^{2b})$.
3. Compare $L$ with $H$ over all pairs of points using the cross-entropy.
4. Minimize it with gradient descent, whose gradient acts as attractive forces along the edges of $H$ and repulsive forces between pairs that are not fully connected.
5. Decrease the learning rate over a fixed number of iterations, so the layout forms globally first and then freezes locally.


# Implementation Details

## Nearest Neighbor Descent

Finding the exact $k$ nearest neighbors of every point means comparing every pair of points, with complexity $O(N^2)$, which becomes troublesome for large datasets. UMAP instead uses an approximate method called **Nearest Neighbor Descent**, based on the idea that a neighbor of a neighbor is likely to be a neighbor too.

Starting from random neighbor lists, it iteratively compares each point only with the neighbors of its current neighbors and keeps any closer candidate, until the lists stall. This brings the complexity close to linear, empirically about $O(N^{1.14})$[^umap-paper].



## Spectral initialization

The optimization needs a starting position for every point. Instead of a random one, UMAP computes a first layout directly from $H$ using **spectral embedding**, also known as **Laplacian eigenmaps**, which already places strongly connected points close together so the optimization only has to refine it[^brunton].

It starts from the **graph Laplacian** $\Delta_H = D - W$, where $W$ is the weight matrix of $H$ with $w_{ij}$ entries, and $D$ is the diagonal matrix with $D_{ii} = \sum_j w_{ij}$[^umap-paper].

Think of every edge of $H$ as a spring with stiffness $w_{ij}$ pulling its two points toward each other. The final layout lives in $\mathbb{R}^d$, but it can be built one axis at a time, so take a vector $u$ that gives each point $i$ a position $u_i$ along a single axis. Then $\Delta_H \cdot u$ gives the total force of the springs on each point along that axis.

An eigenvector of $\Delta_H$ satisfies $\Delta_H u = \lambda u$, which means every point is pulled toward the center with a force equal to $\lambda$ times its distance from it, so the layout would shrink without changing shape. The eigenvalue $\lambda$ is therefore how strong that force is, and a small one means the springs are barely stretched, so strongly connected points sit close together[^brunton]. Therefore, Umap keeps the eigenvectors with the smallest eigenvalues.

The smallest eigenvalue is always zero, a trivial solution whose eigenvector places every point at the same spot, so it is skipped. The eigenvectors with the next $d$ smallest eigenvalues become the $d$ coordinates of each point's position $y_i$.[^brunton].

Starting from this layout makes the optimization converge faster and gives the same starting layout on every run[^statquest].

## Negative sampling

Computing the gradient exactly means summing the forces over every pair of points, which has $O(N^2)$ complexity. 

The attraction is cheap to compute, since it only acts along the edges of $H$ and each point has only around $k$ of them. In each epoch of the stochastic gradient descent, UMAP goes through all the edges of $H$ and, depending on each edge's weight, either pulls its endpoints together or skips it. The strongest edges pull at every epoch, while weaker ones are skipped more often, so they pull less overall[^umap-paper][^umap-learn].

The repulsion is the expensive part, as it acts on almost every pair. UMAP handles it with **negative sampling**. Each time an edge is applied, its first endpoint is also pushed away from a fixed number of randomly drawn points. Since each point has only around $k$ neighbors, a random point is almost never one of them, so these pushes act almost only between unrelated points[^umap-paper][^umap-learn].

As $H$ has at most $kN$ edges, each applied with one pull and a few pushes, the complexity of an epoch drops from $O(N^2)$ to $O(kN)$[^umap-paper].

Because the number of negative samples per edge is fixed, the repulsion on the first endpoint of the edge is much weaker than if it were pushed away from all $N-1$ other points in the data. The forces no longer balance when $v_{ij} = w_{ij}$. For connected pairs attraction dominates, so every pair connected in $H$ is pulled towards a weight close to 1 in $L$. In other words, UMAP effectively preserves which points are connected rather than how strongly. Over the whole dataset, however, this weaker repulsion keeps the total attraction and repulsion evenly matched, which is thought to be the key to UMAP's good results[^damrich].

# Effect of $k$ and min_dist hyperparameters

The two hyperparameters, $k$ and `min_dist`, control the **balance between local and global structure in the final projection**[^pair].

The most influential is the number of neighbors $k$, as it decides how $H$ is shaped, and with it the **balance between local and global structure**. Low values focus UMAP on local structure. Each point only connects to its closest neighbors, so fine detail is kept. The problem is that $H$ can break into disconnected pieces whose relative positions in the projection mean nothing, and even random noise can appear as separate clusters[^umap-docs-params][^pair]. High values push UMAP towards the global structure. Distant parts of the data get connected, so the projection keeps how they relate to each other, at the cost of losing fine detail and being more computationally expensive.

`min_dist` controls **how tightly UMAP packs points together** in the low-dimensional representation. Low values pack neighbors tightly, giving dense clusters with clear gaps between them, which favors local structure[^umap-docs-params]. High values spread points out more evenly, which favors the overall shape of the data, at the cost of smaller gaps between clusters.

# UMAP Weaknesses

- **Spurious clusters.** UMAP's first assumption is that the data lies on a manifold, and it always tries to approximate one, even when there is none. Random noise can therefore appear as clusters in the low-dimensional representation, especially in small datasets and for small values of $k$. UMAP can also split a single group of points into several clusters that look separate in the plot but are connected in the original data. Nothing in the output tells a real cluster from a spurious one[^umap-paper][^pair][^umap-docs-clustering].

- **Meaningless distances.** UMAP aims to preserve the topology of the manifold, which points are connected, not how far apart they are. The axes of the embedding have no meaning, unlike in other dimensionality reduction methods such as PCA, where each axis is a direction of greatest variance in the data. The gaps between clusters don't mean much either, since [attraction and repulsion](#attraction-and-repulsion) only push unconnected points apart and nothing sets how far they end up[^pair]. Where clusters end up relative to each other depends mostly on the [spectral initialization](#spectral-initialization), since the optimization barely moves whole clusters once they are apart[^umap-paper][^kobak].

- **Lost density.** UMAP assumes the data is uniformly distributed on the manifold, so it gives each point its own local distance, set by $\rho_i$ and $\sigma_i$, under which every neighborhood looks equally dense. [Negative sampling](#negative-sampling) adds to this, as it keeps which points are connected rather than how strongly[^damrich]. Dense and sparse regions come out alike, so the size of a cluster in the plot says little about how spread out it is in the data[^umap-paper][^pair].

- **Unstable results.** The embedding changes a lot with $k$ and `min_dist`, and the stochastic optimization gives a different layout on every run, so a single plot shouldn't be trusted on its own[^pair].


# UMAP for Supervised Dimension Reduction and Metric Learning

While UMAP is mostly used for unsupervised dimension reduction, it can also make use of labels when they are available, which turns it into a method for **supervised dimension reduction** and even **metric learning**. The layout optimization stays exactly the same, the labels only affect how the graph $H$ is built[^umap-docs-supervised].

For **categorical labels**, UMAP goes through the edges of $H$ and adjusts each weight according to the labels of the two points it connects. **Edges between points with the same label are preserved**, edges between **different labels are strongly down weighted**, and edges touching a point with an **unknown label**, as in **semi-supervised** settings, are only **moderately down weighted**. Writing $c_i$ for the label of $x_i$, with $c_i = -1$ when it is unknown, the new weights are[^umap-learn]

$$
w_{ij}' = \begin{cases}
w_{ij}\, e^{-d_{\text{unknown}}} & \text{if } c_i = -1 \text{ or } c_j = -1 \\
w_{ij}\, e^{-d_{\text{far}}} & \text{if } c_i \neq c_j \\
w_{ij} & \text{if } c_i = c_j
\end{cases}
$$

where $d_{\text{far}}$ is much larger than $d_{\text{unknown}}$. Note that labels can only weaken existing edges, never create new ones, so two points of the same class that weren't neighbors in $H$ stay unconnected.

Lowering weights can leave a point without any edge of weight 1, which breaks the **local connectivity** assumption from [Edge weights](#edge-weights). UMAP restores it by rescaling the edges of each point so that its strongest one is 1 again. Since every point is rescaled by its own factor, the weights of an edge seen from each of its two points no longer match, so the views are merged once more with the **fuzzy union** from [Merging the local views](#merging-the-local-views)[^umap-learn].

No extra force is needed to separate the classes. Edges between classes barely pull anymore, so the repulsion from [negative sampling](#negative-sampling) pushes them apart like any other unconnected points. Edges within each class are untouched, so the **internal structure of the individual classes** is retained, while the weakened edges between classes still keep some of the **global structure**, so classes that were close in the original data end up close in the embedding space too[^umap-docs-supervised].

Labels don't need to be categories. For a continuous target, as in regression, UMAP builds a second graph from the target values alone, in the same way it built $H$, and keeps only the edges that both graphs agree on, a **fuzzy intersection** that multiplies their weights[^umap-learn]. 
<!-- When predicting house prices, for instance, two similar houses with very different prices lose their edge, so the embedding groups houses that are similar in both features and price. -->

Once fitted, UMAP can also place new, unlabeled points in the embedding. Each new point is connected to its nearest neighbors in $H$ (with edge weights computed as before) and, in the embedding space, its starting position is the weighted average of the positions of those neighbors. Its position is then refined with the same optimization, keeping the original points fixed[^umap-learn]. 

This is what enables **metric learning**, that is, learning a distance from labeled examples. The labels shape an embedding where points of the same class are close and points of different classes are far apart, so distances measured there between new points reflect their classes. However, new points are placed using only their nearest neighbors in the data, not labels. A new point from a region where two classes overlap is connected to both and may land between the two clusters, where the learned distance can't tell which class it belongs to.
